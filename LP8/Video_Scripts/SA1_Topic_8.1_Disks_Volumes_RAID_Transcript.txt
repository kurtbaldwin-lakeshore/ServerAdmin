Welcome to Topic 8.1, Disks, Volumes and RAID, the start of Learning Plan 8. Every file server starts with the same job: turn blank drives into safe, usable storage. You will compare hard drives, SSDs and NVMe drives, and the three ways to connect storage: DAS, NAS and SAN. You will choose MBR or GPT and see why dynamic disks are out. You will pick NTFS or ReFS, create, extend and shrink volumes in Disk Management and PowerShell, and calculate what RAID gives you and what it costs.

Servers use three kinds of drives. A hard disk drive, or HDD, has spinning platters, at 7,200, 10,000 or 15,000 rpm. It is cheap per terabyte but slow, so it suits archives and backups. An SSD stores data in flash memory with no moving parts, so it is much faster. Flash wears out with writes, so SSDs carry an endurance rating. NVMe SSDs talk over PCI Express lanes instead of SATA or SAS, for the lowest delay. Hot-swap bays let you replace a failed drive while the server runs.

Direct-attached storage, or DAS, is inside the server or on a shelf connected with a SAS cable. Only that server uses it. The four data disks on SRV01 are DAS. Network-attached storage, or NAS, is a box that shares folders over the network with SMB or NFS. That is file storage, used in Lab 8.3. A storage area network, or SAN, gives servers whole disks, called LUNs, over iSCSI or Fibre Channel. That is block storage that looks like a local disk, used in Lab 8.4.

Here is the disk you build in the lab. A disk first gets a partition style, MBR or GPT. Then you cut it into partitions. A partition with a file system on it, such as NTFS or ReFS, is a volume. Then you give it a drive letter like E, or a mount point. A mount point makes a volume appear as an empty folder on an NTFS volume, such as E Archive. Windows may also add a small reserved partition with no letter.

MBR, the Master Boot Record, is the old partition style. It can only use 2 terabytes of a disk and holds four primary partitions, or three plus an extended partition with logical drives. GPT, the GUID Partition Table, handles disks far larger than anything sold today and up to 128 partitions in Windows. It keeps a backup copy of its table, and UEFI computers boot from it. GPT is the default in Disk Management and in Initialize-Disk, so choose GPT. Changing later is hard, because Disk Management converts only an empty disk.

Every new disk is a basic disk. It holds partitions and simple volumes, and that is all you need in this course. Dynamic disks are the old way Windows built spanned, striped, mirrored and RAID-5 volumes across several disks. Microsoft now calls dynamic disks deprecated and does not recommend them for new deployments. You may still find them on old servers, and going back to basic means deleting every volume. Instead, pool disks with Storage Spaces, which is Topic 8.2, or use a hardware RAID controller.

Windows Server gives you two main file systems. NTFS is the general-purpose one. It can boot, so C is always NTFS. It can shrink, and it supports disk quotas and removable media. ReFS, the Resilient File System, is built for big data. It handles volumes up to 35 petabytes, against 256 terabytes for NTFS. Block clone copies large files almost instantly, and integrity streams keep checksums of file data. But ReFS cannot boot and cannot shrink. Both support permissions, BitLocker, deduplication and extending. Use NTFS by default, and ReFS for VMs, backups and archives.

Disk Management is the graphical tool. Right-click Start and choose Disk Management. In the lower pane, find the new disk by its size and state: 20 gigabytes, Unknown, Offline. Numbers can change, so do not trust them alone. Right-click the grey label on the left, not the bar, and choose Online. The disk now says Not Initialized. Right-click the label again and choose Initialize Disk. In the dialog, check that only your disk is selected, choose GPT, GUID Partition Table, and select OK. The disk becomes Basic, with unallocated space.

Now right-click the unallocated space and choose New Simple Volume. On Specify Volume Size, the size is in megabytes. Ten gigabytes is 10 times 1024, so type 10240. The next page assigns a drive letter, E in our lab, or a mount point folder. On Format Partition, choose the file system, NTFS here, keep the default allocation unit size, and type a volume label, Data. Leave quick format on. Select Next and Finish, and in a few seconds Data E appears, healthy and ready.

The same job in PowerShell takes a handful of cmdlets. Get-Disk, filtered on partition style RAW, lists the blank disks. Set-Disk with IsOffline false brings the disk online, and IsReadOnly false makes it writable. Initialize-Disk with partition style GPT writes the partition table. Then New-Partition with a size of 10GB and drive letter E creates the partition, and you pipe it straight into Format-Volume with the file system and the label. In the lab a variable holds the disk, so you never mistype its number.

Volumes can grow and shrink. Right-click a volume and choose Extend Volume. The wizard asks how many megabytes to add. The unallocated space must be on the same disk and right after the volume, with nothing in between. NTFS and ReFS volumes can both be extended. Shrink Volume works the other way. Windows first checks how much space it can free, then you type the amount to shrink in megabytes. Only NTFS volumes can shrink, and files that cannot move, such as a page file, set the limit.

In PowerShell, start with Get-PartitionSupportedSize. It shows SizeMin and SizeMax in bytes, the smallest and largest the partition can be right now. Stay between those two values. Resize-Partition with a bigger size extends the volume, here to 12GB. Resize-Partition with a smaller size shrinks it, back to 10GB. To check, read the partition's Size property and divide by 1GB, so you see a simple 12 or 10. PowerShell understands suffixes like GB and MB, while Disk Management always asks in megabytes.

RAID makes several drives act as one, a big Server+ topic. RAID 0 stripes data across drives. It is fast, but if any drive fails, everything is lost. RAID 1 mirrors: two drives hold the same data, so either can fail. RAID 5 stripes data with parity, a calculation that can rebuild a lost block, spread over at least three drives. RAID 6 keeps two parity blocks, so it needs four drives and survives two failures. RAID 10 stripes across mirrored pairs, for speed and safety.

Here are the formulas, where n is the number of drives and s is the size of one. RAID 0 gives you all of it, n times s. RAID 1 gives one drive's worth. RAID 5 gives n minus 1 times s, and RAID 6 n minus 2. RAID 10 gives half. Six 4 terabyte drives give 24, 20, 16 or 12 terabytes. One small write on RAID 5 means reading old data and parity, then writing both: four operations. RAID 6 needs six. RAID 1 and 10 need two.

A hot spare is an extra drive that sits idle. When a drive fails, the array rebuilds onto the spare at once. It adds no capacity. A hardware RAID controller is a card with its own processor and cache, and Windows sees just one disk. In write-back mode, it reports done once data is in its cache. A battery or a flash module protects that cache if power fails. Software RAID, like Storage Spaces, uses the server's CPU. And remember: RAID is not a backup.

Four common mistakes. One: choosing a disk by its number alone. Numbers change, so match the size and the RAW state first. Two: treating RAID as a backup. RAID keeps you running when a drive dies, but a deleted or encrypted file is gone on every drive. Three: using ReFS for C, or for a volume you may need to shrink. ReFS cannot boot or shrink and has no quotas. Four: trying to extend into free space that is not right next to the volume. Plan your layout first.

Time to check yourself. First, a new 6 terabyte disk must hold one 5 terabyte volume. Do you choose MBR or GPT, and why? Second, five 8 terabyte drives are in RAID 5. What is the usable capacity, and how many drives can fail? Third, you want to shrink F, which is formatted with ReFS. What happens? Fourth, a file server must keep running if any two drives fail. Which RAID level do you choose, and how many drives do you need at least? Pause the video and answer.

Here are the answers. One: GPT. MBR can only use 2 terabytes of a disk, while GPT uses all 6, and it is the default anyway. Two: 32 terabytes, and one drive can fail. RAID 5 gives n minus 1 times s, so 4 times 8. Three: you cannot shrink a ReFS volume. Copy the data off, delete the volume and create it again at the new size. Four: RAID 6, with at least four drives. Double parity survives any two failures.

Let me show you on SRV01. In Disk Management, the four new disks show 20 gigabytes, Unknown and Offline, and Get-Disk shows them as RAW. I bring the first one online, initialize it as GPT, and create E, Data, NTFS, 10240 megabytes. Now I extend E by 2 gigabytes, check it shows 12, and shrink it back to 10 with Resize-Partition. Next, PowerShell creates F, Archive, with ReFS. Watch the menus: F cannot shrink, and E can no longer extend, because F sits right after it. E has a Quota tab. F does not.

To wrap up. Hard drives give capacity, SSDs and NVMe give speed, and storage can be DAS, NAS or SAN. Use GPT and basic disks, because dynamic disks are deprecated. Use NTFS for general volumes and C, and ReFS for big data, knowing it cannot boot or shrink. Bring disks online, initialize, partition and format them. Extend needs free space right after the volume. RAID trades capacity for safety, and it is never a backup. Lab 8.1 takes about 75 minutes on SRV01, plus a RAID worksheet.
