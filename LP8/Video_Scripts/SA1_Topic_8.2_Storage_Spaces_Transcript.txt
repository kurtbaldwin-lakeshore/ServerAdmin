Welcome to Topic 8.2, Storage Spaces. In Topic 8.1 you worked with single disks and learned RAID on paper. Now Windows Server does the RAID for you, in software. You will learn the four layers of Storage Spaces and the primordial pool. You will pick a resiliency type from the minimum disks and the usable space it gives. You will choose thin or fixed provisioning, build a pool, virtual disks and volumes in Server Manager and in PowerShell, check their health, and plan what to do when a disk fails.

Storage Spaces has four layers. At the bottom are physical disks, plain blank drives. They do not have to be the same size. You group them into a storage pool, and Windows treats all their space as one. From the pool you carve virtual disks, also called storage spaces. Each one has its own resiliency, such as a mirror or parity, and Windows sees it as an ordinary disk. On top you create volumes, with a drive letter and NTFS or ReFS. In the lab, four 20 GB disks become SA1Pool, with Mirror1 and Parity1 on it.

Before you create any pool, Windows already shows one, called the primordial pool. It is not a real pool. It is the list of every disk that is able to join a pool. When you put disks into a new pool, they leave the primordial pool. If you see no primordial pool, no disk qualifies. A disk qualifies when it is blank, with no partitions, and at least 4 GB. It must not hide behind a RAID controller, so it connects through a simple host bus adapter. And a storage space cannot hold Windows itself.

This table is the heart of the topic. Simple stripes data with no protection: one disk, full capacity, and one failure loses data. A two-way mirror keeps two copies: at least two disks, survives one failure, and you get half the space. A three-way mirror needs five disks and keeps a third. Parity needs three disks, survives one failure and gives two thirds or more. Dual parity needs seven disks and survives two failures. For the Server+ exam, plan capacity: raw space times the usable share, then leave free space.

When you create a virtual disk, you choose how it takes space from the pool. Fixed takes the full size right away. Parity1 in the lab is fixed, so its 10 GB, plus the parity, is reserved at once. Thin only promises the size. Mirror1 says 10 GB, but it takes pool space only as data is written. The catch is overcommit. Thin disks can promise more than the pool really has. Windows warns at 70 percent pool use by default, and if the pool fills, the space goes offline. So watch free space and add disks early.

Where does the speed come from? A virtual disk writes data in stripes across several disks at once. The number of disks one stripe spans is the column count. More columns means more disks working in parallel. The amount written to each column in one stripe is the interleave, 256 KB by default. The wizard chooses both for you. PowerShell can set them with dash NumberOfColumns and dash Interleave. One planning rule: to grow a space later, the pool needs free space on as many disks as the space has columns, times the copies for a mirror.

In Server Manager, open File and Storage Services, then Volumes, then Storage Pools. The page has three tiles. Storage Pools lists the pools. Before you build anything, the only entry is Primordial, under Storage Spaces. The Virtual Disks tile is empty for now. The Physical Disks tile lists the disks of the selected pool. Identify the data disks by their size, 20 GB, never by disk number alone, because numbers can change. The Tasks menu on each tile starts the wizards: New Storage Pool, and later New Virtual Disk.

The New Storage Pool Wizard is short. First you name the pool, SA1Pool, and pick the primordial pool of SRV01 as the source of disks. Then you tick the physical disks, all four 20 GB data disks. Each disk has an Allocation setting. Automatic means it carries data. Hot Spare means it waits unused until another disk fails. In the lab you keep all four on Automatic. Confirm, select Create, and the four disks leave the primordial pool and become SA1Pool.

Next, select the pool and choose New Virtual Disk from the Tasks menu of the Virtual Disks tile. Name it Mirror1. On the Storage Layout page you pick Simple, Mirror or Parity. With only four disks, Mirror always means a two-way mirror. The page that lets you choose a three-way mirror appears only when the pool has five or more disks. Then choose Thin provisioning and a size of 10 GB. On the last page, keep Create a volume when this wizard closes ticked, so the next wizard opens by itself.

The New Volume Wizard is the same one you met in Topic 8.1, because a virtual disk looks like any other disk. Pick SRV01 and the virtual disk Mirror1. Keep the maximum size. Assign the drive letter M. On File System Settings choose NTFS or ReFS, keep the default allocation unit size, and type the label Mirror. Leave short file names off. When you select Create, the wizard brings the disk online, initializes it, creates the partition and formats it.

Now the same work in PowerShell. Get-PhysicalDisk dash CanPool true lists only the disks that are free to pool. Save them in a variable, then New-StoragePool creates SA1Pool from those disks in the Windows Storage subsystem. The pool reports Healthy and OK. New-VirtualDisk creates a space: give it the pool, a name, the resiliency setting and the provisioning type. Mirror1 is a mirror, thin, 10 GB. Parity1 is parity, fixed, 10 GB. The sizes in the output depend on your pod.

A new virtual disk still needs a volume. Use the pipeline from Topic 8.1. Get-VirtualDisk finds Parity1, Get-Disk turns it into a disk, Initialize-Disk makes it GPT, New-Partition gives it the letter P, and Format-Volume formats it with ReFS and the label Parity. Then check health. Get-VirtualDisk shows each space with its resiliency, provisioning, HealthStatus and OperationalStatus. Healthy and OK are what you want. Get-StoragePool shows the same for the pool. There is also New-Volume, which does everything in one step.

What happens when a disk fails? The virtual disk shows HealthStatus Warning and OperationalStatus Degraded, but your data is still there. Now follow the steps in order. Add a new disk to the pool, or let a hot spare step in. Retire the failed disk with Set-PhysicalDisk dash Usage Retired, so nothing new lands on it. Run Repair-VirtualDisk and wait for the job, which you can watch with Get-StorageJob. Only then remove the disk and pull it. A repair needs free pool space. In the pod, this is concept only.

How does Storage Spaces compare with a hardware RAID controller? Storage Spaces runs in Windows, takes disks of any size and lets you add more later, with thin provisioning and tiers. Hardware RAID runs on a controller card, usually wants matching disks, and often has a battery-backed or flash-backed cache. Hardware RAID can hold the boot disk; Storage Spaces cannot. Do not stack them: Storage Spaces wants plain disks. A common design is two disks in hardware RAID 1 for Windows, and the data disks in Storage Spaces.

Two more ideas, as concepts only. Storage tiers mix fast SSDs and slower hard disks in one virtual disk. Windows moves busy data to the SSD tier and quiet data to the hard disks. Tiers need fixed provisioning. Storage Spaces Direct goes further. A failover cluster of 2 to 16 servers pools the local drives of every server over the network, so storage survives a whole server failing. It needs Datacenter edition and is the base of Azure Local. The AZ-802 exam covers it; our single-server pod cannot.

Four common mistakes. One: picking disks by number. Numbers change, so pick by size and blank state, or let Get-PhysicalDisk dash CanPool true choose. Two: using thin disks without watching the pool. They can overcommit it, so watch free space and add disks before it fills. Three: parity for busy servers. Parity writes are slow, so use a mirror for virtual machines and busy shares, and parity for archive and backup. Four: pulling a failed disk first. Add, retire, repair and wait, and only then remove it.

Time to check yourself. SA1Pool has four 20 GB disks. First, about how much data fits in one two-way mirror that uses the whole pool? Second, why does the wizard not offer a three-way mirror for SA1Pool? Third, Mirror1 is thin and 10 GB. Why does the pool show much less used space than 10 GB times two? Fourth, Get-VirtualDisk shows Mirror1 as Warning and Degraded. What happened, and what do you do? Pause the video and answer all four before you go on.

Here are the answers. One: about 40 GB. A two-way mirror stores everything twice, so 80 GB of raw space gives half, minus a little for pool metadata. Two: a three-way mirror needs at least five disks, and SA1Pool has four. Three: a thin disk takes pool space only as data is written, so an empty one uses almost nothing. Four: a disk failed, but the data is still there. Add a new disk, retire the failed one, run Repair-VirtualDisk, wait for it to finish, and then remove the old disk.

Let me show you on SRV01. Under Disks you see the four blank 20 GB disks, and under Storage Pools the primordial pool. I run the New Storage Pool Wizard, name it SA1Pool and tick all four disks. Then the New Virtual Disk Wizard makes Mirror1: Mirror layout, notice there is no three-way choice, thin, 10 GB. The New Volume Wizard gives it M and the label Mirror. In PowerShell, New-VirtualDisk makes Parity1, fixed, and the disk pipeline formats it as P with ReFS. Finally Get-VirtualDisk shows both spaces Healthy and OK.

To wrap up. Blank disks go into a pool, virtual disks or spaces are carved from it, and volumes sit on top. A two-way mirror needs two disks, a three-way mirror five, parity three and dual parity seven. Usable space is raw space times the usable share, and you keep free space for repairs. Thin grows as it is used; fixed takes its space now. Check HealthStatus and OperationalStatus, and when a disk fails, retire it, then repair. In Lab 8.2 you build all of this on SRV01 in about 70 minutes.
