Welcome to Topic 4.4, Read-Only DCs and Hybrid Identity. So far, sa1.lab has one domain controller, DC01. In this topic you will see why a domain needs at least two. You will meet the read-only domain controller, or RODC, and learn what it stores and why branch offices use one. You will promote SRV-CORE to an RODC with PowerShell, and manage its Password Replication Policy in ADUC and PowerShell. Then we look at the cloud: Microsoft Entra ID, the two sync tools, the sign-in options, and why the UPN suffix matters.

With one domain controller, everything depends on one server. If DC01 fails, nobody can sign in, and the AD-integrated DNS zones are gone too. That is a single point of failure. Real domains run at least two writable DCs. Each holds a full copy of AD DS, accepts changes, and replicates them to the others. If one fails, the other carries on. On a second joined server, you would install the AD DS role, then run Install-ADDSDomainController with the domain name. Our pod has one writable DC, so this is shown, not done.

Now picture a branch office. A few users, a slow link to the main campus, a server in a closet that anyone could walk off with, and nobody from IT on site. A writable DC there is risky. A read-only domain controller fits better. It holds every object and attribute, except account passwords. By default it stores only its own computer account and its own krbtgt account. Replication is one-way: nothing replicates out of an RODC, so a change made there cannot spread. Write requests are referred to a writable DC.

So how does anyone sign in at the branch? Follow dpatel. He signs in at SRV-CORE, but SRV-CORE has no copy of his password. So it passes the request over the link to DC01, a writable DC. DC01 signs him in and checks the Password Replication Policy, the PRP, for SRV-CORE. If dpatel is allowed, SRV-CORE stores his password, and next time it checks him itself, even if the link is down. If he is not allowed, nothing is stored, and every sign-in needs DC01. By default, no user passwords are cached.

The PRP has two layers. Two domain-wide groups sit in the Users container. The Allowed RODC Password Replication Group starts empty. The Denied RODC Password Replication Group holds powerful groups like Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins and Schema Admins. Each RODC also has its own Allowed list and Denied list. By default its Allowed list holds the allowed group, and its Denied list holds the denied group plus Administrators and three operator groups. Three rules. Deny wins. The group affects every RODC, the list just one. And allowed means may be cached, not cached yet.

Two more RODC features. Administrator role separation lets you make a branch user or group the local administrator of one RODC. They can update a driver or restart the server, but they get no rights in the domain or on other DCs. You set it with the DelegatedAdministratorAccountName parameter or the Managed By tab. Second, read-only DNS. With dash InstallDns, the RODC holds a read-only copy of zones like sa1.lab, so branch clients resolve names locally. It does not accept client updates; it refers them to DC01. And in real life, never sign in to an RODC as a Domain Admin.

SRV-CORE is Server Core, so this is PowerShell from SConfig option fifteen. First, Install-WindowsFeature AD-Domain-Services with dash IncludeManagementTools. Then Install-ADDSDomainController. Dash DomainName sa1.lab, and the key switch, dash ReadOnlyReplica. With it, dash SiteName is required, so we give Default-First-Site-Name. Dash InstallDns adds the read-only DNS server, and dash Credential with Get-Credential SA1 Administrator opens a sign-in box for that password. Then you type the DSRM password twice, masked. In the pod, both are the password shown in your NetLab pod. Answer Y, and SRV-CORE restarts as an RODC.

Back on DC01, open Active Directory Users and Computers. Select Domain Controllers, right-click SRV-CORE, and choose Properties. The Password Replication Policy tab lists every group with its setting, Allow or Deny. You see the defaults: the allowed group, and five denied entries. Select Add. A small dialog asks whether to allow or deny. Choose Allow passwords for the account to replicate to this RODC, then type GG-Students, Check Names, and OK. GG-Students now shows Allow, but only on SRV-CORE, not on every RODC in the domain.

On the same tab, select Advanced. The Policy Usage tab has a drop-down. Accounts whose passwords are stored on this Read-only Domain Controller shows what is really cached. At first, only the RODC's own krbtgt account and its computer account. The other choice shows accounts that have been authenticated through the RODC. Prepopulate Passwords lets you cache an allowed user before they ever sign in, which is handy before a branch opens. And the Resultant Policy tab answers one question for one account: allow or deny?

Everything you saw in ADUC has a PowerShell form. Get-ADDomainController with dash Filter star shows both DCs; SRV-CORE has IsReadOnly True, and it is a global catalog too. Add-ADDomainControllerPasswordReplicationPolicy with dash AllowedList GG-Students does the same as Add in ADUC. Get-ADDomainControllerPasswordReplicationPolicy with dash Allowed or dash Denied reads the two lists. And Get-ADDomainControllerPasswordReplicationPolicyUsage with dash RevealedAccounts lists the passwords that are actually stored. The names are long, so type the first part and press Tab to complete them. Run them on DC01, where the AD module is installed.

Now the cloud side. AD DS runs on your own domain controllers and uses Kerberos, NTLM and LDAP. Microsoft Entra ID is a Microsoft cloud service. It speaks web protocols such as SAML and OAuth 2.0. It has no OUs or Group Policy; administration uses built-in roles. Devices can be Entra joined and managed with Intune. Entra ID is what Microsoft 365 and many cloud apps use. Its old name was Azure Active Directory. Hybrid identity means one account works in both, because a sync tool copies users and groups from AD DS to Entra ID.

There are two sync tools. Microsoft Entra Connect Sync is a full sync server you install on Windows Server, with its own database. It can also set up pass-through authentication and federation. You must keep it current; Learn currently says version 2.6.84.0 or later, with application-based authentication, by April 7, 2027. Microsoft Entra Cloud Sync uses light agents, and its settings live in the Entra admin center. It handles disconnected forests, and Microsoft calls it the recommended path forward for most organizations. In SA1 you see this in my demo video; hands-on Azure comes in Server Administration 2.

How does Entra ID check a password? Three options. Password hash sync, PHS, sends a hash of each password hash to Entra ID, and Entra ID checks sign-ins itself. If the head office goes offline, cloud sign-in still works. Pass-through authentication, PTA, uses agents on your servers to check passwords against AD DS, so the check stays on-premises. Federation hands the whole sign-in to your own AD FS servers. With PTA or federation, if the head office is offline, sign-in fails. That is why Microsoft advises turning on PHS whatever you choose, as a backup.

Ann Lee signs in as alee at sa1.lab. That is her user principal name, or UPN. Entra Connect only syncs users to domains verified in the tenant, and only a real internet domain can be verified. sa1.lab is not one, so a synced user would land on an onmicrosoft dot com name. A real organization first adds a routable domain it owns, for example example.com, as an alternative UPN suffix in Active Directory Domains and Trusts. Then it verifies the domain in Entra ID, changes each user's UPN, and only then syncs.

Four mistakes. One: leaving out dash ReadOnlyReplica. You get a writable DC instead, so read the command before you press Enter. Two: allowing Domain Admins so admins can sign in offline. Deny wins anyway, and you would expose your most powerful passwords; delegate a branch admin group instead. Three: expecting a password on the RODC right after you allow the group. Allowed means may be cached, so prepopulate or wait for a sign-in, then check the stored list. Four: syncing users with an at sa1.lab UPN. Add and verify a routable suffix first.

Time to check yourself. SRV-CORE is an RODC, and GG-Students is on its Allowed list. First, a branch has twelve users, an unlocked closet and a slow link. Writable DC or RODC, and why? Second, a Domain Admins member signs in at SRV-CORE. Is the password cached? Third, dpatel is in GG-Students, but the stored list does not show him. Why, and how do you fix it now? And fourth, cloud sign-in must keep working when the head office is offline. Which sign-in option? Pause the video now.

Here are the answers. One: an RODC. With weak physical security and a slow link, you want a DC that nothing replicates out of, and that stores no passwords unless you allow them. Two: no. Domain Admins is in the Denied RODC Password Replication Group, and deny wins, so DC01 checks every one of those sign-ins. Three: allowed does not mean cached yet. Use Prepopulate Passwords, or wait for his first sign-in through SRV-CORE. Four: password hash sync, because Entra ID checks the password itself.

On SRV-CORE, I install the AD DS role and run Install-ADDSDomainController with dash ReadOnlyReplica and dash SiteName. I type the passwords at the prompts and answer Y. After the restart, DC01 lists SRV-CORE with IsReadOnly True. In ADUC, the Password Replication Policy tab shows the defaults. I add GG-Students with Allow. Under Advanced, only the RODC's own accounts are stored. The Resultant Policy allows dpatel but not alee. I prepopulate dpatel, and PowerShell confirms his password is now stored on SRV-CORE. Nobody else from GG-Students is cached yet, and that is exactly what the policy promised.

To wrap up. Two writable DCs remove the single point of failure. An RODC holds every object but no passwords by default, and nothing replicates out of it. Install-ADDSDomainController with dash ReadOnlyReplica and dash SiteName builds one. In the PRP, deny wins, and allowed means may be cached. Entra Connect Sync or Cloud Sync copies AD DS users to Entra ID. Turn on password hash sync as a backup, and use a routable UPN suffix.
