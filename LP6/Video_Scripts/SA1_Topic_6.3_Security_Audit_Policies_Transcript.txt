Welcome to Topic 6.3, Security and Audit Policies. In the last two topics you created, linked and filtered GPOs. Now you use them to harden the domain. You will see why domain password and lockout rules must come from the domain itself, and you will set them in the Default Domain Policy. You will give the IT group stricter rules with a fine-grained password policy. You will stop students from signing in at server consoles. And you will turn on auditing, lock out a test account on purpose, find the events it leaves, and unlock it.

Account policies come in three groups. The password policy says what a good password is: how long, how complex, how often it may be reused, and how old it may get. The defaults are a history of 24, a maximum age of 42 days, and a minimum length of only 7. The lockout policy decides when to lock an account after bad guesses. By default the threshold is 0, so accounts never lock. The Kerberos policy sets how long sign-in tickets last, 10 hours for a user ticket, and allows a clock difference of 5 minutes.

Here is the rule that surprises many people. Domain accounts are checked by domain controllers, and domain controllers only use account policies from GPOs linked at the domain. That is the Default Domain Policy. If you put a password policy in a GPO linked to OU Workstations, CLIENT01 applies it, but only to its own local accounts, like the local student account. Domain users such as alee or emoss are not affected at all. So there is one domain password policy. If one group needs different rules, you use a fine-grained password policy instead.

What should those rules be? Microsoft's current guidance has changed a lot. Length matters more than complexity, and Microsoft recommends a minimum of 14 characters. Passphrases help, because long phrases are easy to remember. Forced expiration is discouraged. Change a password when it may be compromised, not every few weeks. Ban the obvious passwords, and add multifactor authentication. In our pod, we use a minimum of 12 and lock after 5 bad attempts for 15 minutes. And remember: a new rule is checked only when a password is set or changed. Existing passwords keep working.

Let's make the change. In Group Policy Management, under Domains and sa1.lab, right-click Default Domain Policy and choose Edit. In the Group Policy Management Editor, go to Computer Configuration, Policies, Windows Settings, Security Settings, Account Policies. Select Password Policy, double-click Minimum password length, and type 12. The box accepts 0 to 14 by default. Then select Account Lockout Policy. Set the threshold to 5. Windows suggests values for the other two settings; set the duration and the reset counter to 15 minutes each.

Now check the result on DC01. First, gpupdate with dash force, so DC01 applies the changed GPO right away. Then run Get-ADDefaultDomainPasswordPolicy. Look for LockoutThreshold 5, LockoutDuration and LockoutObservationWindow both 15 minutes, and MinPasswordLength 12. The observation window is the reset counter setting. You may notice a Set-ADDefaultDomainPasswordPolicy cmdlet too. It works, but Group Policy writes these values from the GPO and can overwrite your direct change. So keep the Default Domain Policy as the one place you edit, and check with this cmdlet afterward.

What if the IT staff should use longer passwords than everyone else? That is the job of a fine-grained password policy, stored as a Password Settings Object, or PSO. Our PSO-IT has precedence 10 and a minimum length of 15. It applies to the global group GG-IT, so alee, a member, must use 15 characters. Everyone else, with no PSO, keeps the domain rules of 12. Three rules to remember. PSOs apply to users and global security groups, never to OUs. The lowest precedence number wins. And a PSO linked to a user directly beats any group PSO.

You create a PSO in Active Directory Administrative Center. Select the domain, open System, then Password Settings Container, and in the Tasks pane choose New, Password Settings. Type the name PSO-IT and precedence 10. Under Enforce minimum password length, type 15. Keep the password history and complexity. Fill the password age options and turn on the lockout policy with the same 5, 15 and 15 as the domain. Last, under Directly Applies To, select Add and choose GG-IT. Select OK, and the PSO is live.

The same PSO in PowerShell takes two cmdlets. New-ADFineGrainedPasswordPolicy creates PSO-IT, with every value you saw in the dialog: precedence, length, history, complexity, ages and lockout. Add-ADFineGrainedPasswordPolicySubject then applies it to GG-IT. To see which policy really applies to a user, run Get-ADUserResultantPasswordPolicy. For alee it returns PSO-IT, precedence 10, minimum length 15. For dpatel it returns nothing at all. An empty result means no PSO applies, so dpatel gets the Default Domain Policy. In the lab you create the PSO once, in the Administrative Center.

User rights decide who may sign in where. Allow log on locally lets you sign in at the console. On member servers, that includes the Users group, so any domain user could sign in at SRV01. Allow log on through Remote Desktop Services covers Remote Desktop. Each right has a Deny partner, and Deny wins over Allow. In the new GPO SA1 Server Rights, linked to OU Servers, open Deny log on locally, select Define these policy settings, and add SA1 backslash GG-Students. Students can no longer sign in at any server console.

Now auditing. Windows has two kinds of audit policy. Basic audit policy has 9 broad categories, and one switch turns on a whole category. Advanced audit policy splits 10 categories into subcategories, so you audit only what you need. Use advanced. A Security Option controls this: Audit, Force audit policy subcategory settings. It is enabled by default, so advanced settings win. Just do not mix the two kinds. This is security hardening, as in Server Plus domain 3. Auditing adds accountability, the accounting A in AAA from Information Security Principles.

In the GPO SA1 Workstation Audit, linked to OU Workstations, go to Security Settings, Advanced Audit Policy Configuration, Audit Policies. Under Logon and Logoff, double-click Audit Logon. Select Configure the following audit events, then Success and Failure. Success gives event 4624, failure gives 4625. Then under Account Management, set Audit User Account Management to Success. That records accounts being created, changed, locked out and unlocked. Recent Windows versions already audit some of this, but the GPO makes it certain on every workstation.

The GroupPolicy module creates and links GPOs. New-GPO with a name, piped to New-GPLink with a target OU, does both in one line: SA1 Server Rights on Servers, and SA1 Workstation Audit on Workstations. Get-GPInheritance shows what is linked to an OU. The security settings themselves you still add in the editor. Then on CLIENT01, run gpupdate dash force and auditpol slash get slash category star. Auditpol shows the audit policy the computer really uses: Logon, Success and Failure, and User Account Management, Success.

Picture the lockout test. emoss types a wrong password five times at CLIENT01. Each failure writes event 4625, an account failed to log on, in the Security log of CLIENT01. DC01 counts the bad attempts and, at five, locks the account and writes event 4740. 4740 is logged on the DC that processed the lockout; with several DCs, check the PDC emulator first. In the pod DC01 is both. The account unlocks itself after 15 minutes, or an admin unlocks it, which writes 4767. Learn 4624, 4625, 4740 and 4720.

On DC01, first ask which DC holds the PDC emulator role. Get-ADDomain says DC01. Then Get-WinEvent with a filter for the Security log and ID 4740 returns the newest lockout. Two fields matter: the account that was locked out, emoss, and Caller Computer Name, CLIENT01. That tells you where the bad passwords came from. Search-ADAccount dash LockedOut lists every locked account. Unlock-ADAccount emoss unlocks it. In the GUI, open the user's properties in Active Directory Users and Computers and use the Account tab.

Here are four mistakes to avoid. One: setting password length or lockout in a GPO linked to an OU. Domain accounts ignore it; use the Default Domain Policy, or a PSO. Two: expecting a new minimum length to change existing passwords. It is only checked when a password is set or changed. Three: applying a PSO to an OU or to a domain local group. PSOs apply to users and global security groups. Four: mixing basic and advanced audit policy. Use advanced only and check the result with auditpol.

Time to check yourself. Assume the Lab 6.3 settings: minimum 12, lockout after 5, and PSO-IT for GG-IT. First, a GPO on OU Staff sets minimum length 14. Why can cdiaz still choose a 12-character password? Second, PSO-IT reaches alee through GG-IT, and another PSO with precedence 20 is linked to alee directly. Which wins? Third, dpatel cannot sign in at the SRV01 console but can at CLIENT01. Why? Fourth, which event, on which computer, shows where the bad passwords for emoss came from? Pause and answer.

Here are the answers. One: domain accounts use only the domain policy. The GPO on OU Staff would only touch local accounts, so cdiaz gets the minimum of 12. Two: the PSO linked directly to alee wins, whatever the precedence numbers. Three: SA1 Server Rights is linked only to OU Servers, where it denies GG-Students console sign-in. CLIENT01 is in OU Workstations. Four: event 4740 on DC01, the PDC emulator. Its Caller Computer Name says CLIENT01, which also has a 4625 for each failed try.

Let's do it in the pod. On DC01 I edit the Default Domain Policy: minimum length 12, threshold 5, and 15 minutes for the duration and the reset. After gpupdate, Get-ADDefaultDomainPasswordPolicy shows the new values. In the Administrative Center I create PSO-IT for GG-IT and check alee. Then I link SA1 Workstation Audit to Workstations with Audit Logon and User Account Management. On CLIENT01, auditpol confirms it. I type a wrong password for emoss five times, and she is locked out. CLIENT01 shows 4625, DC01 shows 4740, and I unlock her.

To wrap up. Domain password and lockout rules come from a GPO linked at the domain, the Default Domain Policy, and new length rules apply at the next password change. A PSO gives a global group stricter rules, and the lowest precedence wins. Deny log on locally beats Allow, so link it where it belongs. Use advanced audit policy for logons and account management. Look for 4625 on the computer and 4740 on the PDC emulator. In Lab 6.3, about 75 minutes and practice only, you do all of this in your pod.
